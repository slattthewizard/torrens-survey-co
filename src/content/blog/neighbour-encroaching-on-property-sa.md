The extension next door has gone up and the eaves look like they hang over your side. Or the neighbour's driveway seems a little wider every time it's resurfaced. Before you knock on the door, it helps to know what counts as an encroachment and what's only a hunch.

This article explains what an encroachment is, how to find out whether you've got one, and where disputes end up in South Australia. It's general information, not legal advice.

## What counts as an encroachment

SA Government defines it this way: "An encroachment is when a structure intrudes onto another person's land, crossing the legal boundary. It can include buildings, driveways, eaves and balconies."

Two parts of that sentence matter. One is "structure": a tree dropping leaves into your yard isn't what the definition is about. The other is "crossing the legal boundary". The test isn't whether something feels too close. It's whether it actually crosses a line that only measurement can pin down.

That's also why the fence doesn't settle it. SA Government says a fence's existing location does not change the legal boundary, who legally owns the land, or who owns the fence. If the neighbour's shed sits on the fence line, you know where the shed is. You don't yet know whether it's over.

## Why looking isn't proof

Most owners reach for the same shortcuts: eyeball the fence, compare the title plan to the ground, or check an online map or aerial photo. None of these shows the legal boundary. SA Government says "The boundary can only be determined by a licensed surveyor."

The Surveyors Board SA adds a warning that fits here: "Major discrepancies between boundaries and fencing are not uncommon and can sometimes be very difficult to rectify." It also says surveys by unlicensed practitioners "do not have any legal standing."

So the first practical step isn't a letter. It's finding out where the line is. Our guide to [how to find property boundaries in SA](/blog/how-to-find-property-boundaries-sa/) explains why each shortcut falls short.

## Proving it with a boundary survey

SA Government's advice is to "hire a licensed surveyor to mark the legal boundary to confirm if an encroachment has occurred."

In practice that's a boundary survey, usually an identification survey. The Licensed Surveyor reads the title and registered plan for your block and the neighbours', looks for surviving evidence on the ground, measures to work out where the corners are, places marks at them and records the survey. Under the Survey Act 1992, only a licensed surveyor, or someone working under their supervision, may place the marks. The Board puts it plainly: "In South Australia, only licensed surveyors can undertake a boundary survey or place a survey mark that defines a property boundary."

Two things to keep in mind. The marks "simply represent the surveyor's opinion as to the position of the boundary", according to the Surveyor-General's guidelines, so it's a professional opinion built on measurement. And once marks are in, you can't move or remove them. SA Government says doing so can bring legal action and fines.

A survey also tells you things you didn't ask. If a structure is over the line, you'll know by how much. If it isn't, you'll know that too, which can end a long argument before it starts. More on [boundary surveys](/boundary-surveys/), and on how the same work applies when you're [buying a property](/blog/identification-survey-before-buying/).

If you're the one who has built close to a boundary, the same logic applies in reverse. The Board recommends a Licensed Surveyor to verify your title dimensions and boundary location when building on or near a land boundary, and it's easier to find out before the slab is poured than after.

## Where encroachment disputes go

We aren't giving legal advice here. What we can do is set out what government sources say.

SA Government's disputes page lists "Supreme Court – encroachment disputes" and "Magistrates Court – fencing disputes", so the two go to different courts. The Surveyor-General's guidelines say section 4 of the Encroachments Act 1944 "confers upon both the adjacent owner and an encroaching owner the right to apply to the Land and Valuation Court for relief." That works in both directions: the owner who's been encroached on and the owner who has encroached can each apply.

The Surveyors Board warns that "A boundary dispute can result in expensive litigation, particularly if major improvements you are undertaking need to be removed or relocated." That's the reason most people try to avoid court.

The usual advice starts with talking. SA Government points to Community Mediation Services and the Legal Services Commission for disputes, and a solicitor can tell you where you stand under the Act. Mediation and advice come before a court application, not after.

## A sensible order of steps

1. Hold off on confronting anyone. A hunch isn't evidence.
2. Photograph the structure and note where it sits against the fence.
3. Pull out your certificate of title and plan. Our page on the [certificate of title](/blog/certificate-of-title-sa/) explains what they do and don't show.
4. Have a Licensed Surveyor mark the boundary, and check their licence with the Registrar at registrar@isasa.org.au or on (08) 8212 0343.
5. With the result in hand, talk to your neighbour. If you still disagree, contact the Legal Services Commission, Community Mediation Services or a solicitor.

If the survey finds nothing over the line, you've paid for a survey and gained certainty.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What counts as an encroachment in South Australia?

SA Government says an encroachment is when a structure intrudes onto another person's land, crossing the legal boundary, and can include buildings, driveways, eaves and balconies. Whether a structure crosses the line is a measurement question that only a licensed surveyor can answer.

### How do I prove my neighbour is on my land?

SA Government's advice is to hire a licensed surveyor to mark the legal boundary and confirm whether an encroachment has occurred. A fence, a title plan or an aerial photo doesn't show the legal boundary, so none of those is proof either way.

### Which court deals with encroachments?

SA Government lists the Supreme Court for encroachment disputes and the Magistrates Court for fencing disputes. The Surveyor-General's guidelines also say section 4 of the Encroachments Act 1944 lets both owners apply to the Land and Valuation Court for relief. Which applies to you is a question for a solicitor or the Legal Services Commission.

### Should I try mediation first?

SA Government points to Community Mediation Services and the Legal Services Commission for disputes, and the Surveyors Board warns that boundary litigation can be expensive. Having the boundary marked first gives both sides the same fact to work from, which makes the conversation easier.
