# Permanent survey marks in South Australia: what they are and what you can't do

You've spotted a small metal plug in the footpath outside your place, or a concreter has hit something odd while digging out the driveway. It might be a permanent survey mark, usually shortened to PSM. It looks like a boundary marker. It isn't one, and there's a fine for interfering with it.

This article covers what PSMs are, how they differ from the pegs at your boundary corners, and what to do if one is in the way of your plans. It's general information, not legal advice.

## What a permanent survey mark is

A PSM is a fixed reference point that surveyors tie their measurements back to. You'll mostly find them in public places like footpaths, kerbs and roads, not at the corner of a private block.

SA Government explains how they appear on paper: "PSMs are shown on survey plans with references to the boundary corner but do not mark the boundary corner." Hold onto that sentence. A PSM can sit a few metres from your corner and still tell you nothing, by itself, about where your boundary runs.

The plan records the relationship between the mark and the corner. A surveyor reads that relationship, measures from the mark and works out where the corner is. A tape measure and a guess won't do it.

## PSMs and boundary pegs do different jobs

This table sets the two side by side.

| | Permanent survey mark | Boundary peg or mark |
|---|---|---|
| Where it usually is | Footpaths, kerbs, roads | Corners of your block |
| What it does | Reference point for measurements | Marks a boundary corner |
| Does it mark the boundary? | No, plans link it to the corner | Placed on the surveyor's opinion of the corner |
| Who may place one | A licensed surveyor, or someone they supervise | A licensed surveyor, or someone they supervise |

Under the Survey Act 1992 (SA), a person must not personally place a survey mark on or in land unless they're a licensed surveyor, they're acting under a licensed surveyor's supervision, or the mark is placed during an approved training course. So you can't swap in a marker of your own, either. Our article on [missing survey pegs](/blog/survey-pegs-missing/) covers the boundary side.

## The $2,000 fine and the Surveyor-General

SA Government's wording is blunt: "PSMs must not be disturbed without approval of the Surveyor-General. Fines of up to $2000 per PSM can be applied if they are: disturbed, damaged, moved or removed, destroyed."

The fine is per mark, not per job. And the list goes beyond pulling one out. Damaging a mark while you're doing other work counts too.

The Survey Act also has a section headed "Interference with survey marks" (section 52). We haven't reproduced its text here, and we can't tell you how it applies to your job. If you're unsure whether your work could touch a mark, ask before the digger turns up.

The same goes for the marks on your own boundary. SA Government says "By law, you cannot move or remove a survey mark made by a licenced surveyor."

## Why a surveyor might break up your footpath

A PSM can be buried under a surface, and the surveyor needs to find it. SA Government says "By law, surveyors can break any surface to locate a marker, including driveways, footpaths and landscaped areas."

The Survey Act backs this up. A surveyor, or someone the surveyor has authorised in writing, may enter land at any reasonable time for work connected with a survey, and may take the action needed to carry it out effectively. That includes breaking the surface of land "to the extent necessary to uncover or place a survey mark."

So if a surveyor is poking around the verge outside your house, it may have nothing to do with your block. And if one needs to break a surface on your land to reach a mark, the Act gives them the power, with "to the extent necessary" as the limit. If you're worried about a particular paved area, say so before the day. Our piece on [what a land surveyor does](/blog/what-does-a-land-surveyor-do/) gives more background.

## If a PSM is in your driveway or footpath works

This is where owners and builders get caught. A new crossover, a retaining wall, a footpath replacement or a stormwater trench can all put a mark at risk. Here's a sensible order of events.

1. Look before you dig. Check your plans for a PSM near the site, and look at the kerb, footpath and road edge, not only your own block.
2. Don't dig it out, cover it or pave over it. Photograph it and note where it is.
3. If it sits where your work is going, the decision isn't yours or your builder's. SA Government says PSMs must not be disturbed without the Surveyor-General's approval.
4. Contact the Surveyor-General's Office. SA Government publishes the email DHUD.SurveyOperations@sa.gov.au and the phone number (08) 7133 2300. See its [page on permanent survey marks](https://www.sa.gov.au/topics/housing/owning-a-property/property-boundaries/permanent-survey-marks).
5. Tell your builder or concreter in writing, before they start, where the mark is.

What the Surveyor-General's Office will decide on a particular job, we can't say. We only know the rule: no disturbance without approval.

## When you need a surveyor, not just the mark

Finding a PSM doesn't answer a boundary question. SA Government says "A property boundary defines legal ownership of land and its position can only be determined by a licensed surveyor."

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a permanent survey mark the same as my boundary peg?

No. A PSM is a reference point, usually in a footpath, kerb or road. SA Government says PSMs are shown on survey plans with references to the boundary corner but do not mark the boundary corner. A surveyor uses the mark as one input when working out where your corner sits.

### What's the fine for removing a permanent survey mark?

SA Government says fines of up to $2000 per PSM can apply if a mark is disturbed, damaged, moved, removed or destroyed. It also says PSMs must not be disturbed without the Surveyor-General's approval, so ask first if your work could affect one.

### Can a surveyor break my driveway to find a survey mark?

SA Government says surveyors can break any surface to locate a marker, including driveways, footpaths and landscaped areas. The Survey Act lets a surveyor, or someone authorised in writing by one, enter land at a reasonable time and break the surface to the extent necessary to uncover or place a mark.

### Who do I contact about a survey mark in my way?

SA Government lists the Surveyor-General's Office at DHUD.SurveyOperations@sa.gov.au and (08) 7133 2300. Contact them before any work that could disturb the mark. For a boundary question, a Licensed Surveyor is the person to ask.
