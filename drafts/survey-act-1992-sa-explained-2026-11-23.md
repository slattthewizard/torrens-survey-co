# The Survey Act 1992 (SA) explained for property owners

If you own land in South Australia, one short Act sits behind every boundary peg on your block. The Survey Act 1992 decides who may measure your boundaries for money, who may put a mark in the ground, and what happens when someone pretends to be qualified.

You don't need to read the statute to hire a surveyor. This tour covers the parts an owner is most likely to meet, taken from the Act and the Survey Regulations 2020. It's general information, not legal advice, so for a specific problem talk to a solicitor or the Legal Services Commission.

## What the Act is for

The long title says it's an Act "to provide for the licensing and registration of surveyors and to make provisions relating to surveying and land boundaries". In practice it does two things. It controls who can call themselves a licensed or registered surveyor, and it controls who can do certain survey work.

The Regulations fill in the detail: licence and registration requirements, how surveys must be carried out, and a Code of Ethics.

## The key definitions

Two definitions in section 4 matter most to owners.

A cadastral survey is "any process of determining the boundaries of land by the measurement of distances and angles (including measurement by means of an electronic device) or by photogrammetry". That's the boundary-defining kind, and it's the one the licensing rules are built around.

A survey is wider. It covers a cadastral survey and also any process of working out the form of land, or the position of a point, object, structure or feature on or in land, by measuring distances and angles. So a feature and level survey is a survey, but it isn't a cadastral survey.

The Act also separates a licensed surveyor, who holds a licence, from a registered surveyor, who is registered. A person with a licence is taken to be registered as well. See [licensed vs registered surveyors](/blog/licensed-surveyor-vs-registered-surveyor-sa/) for what that means in practice.

## Who may do what (sections 14 to 18)

These sections are the heart of the Act for an owner. Each carries a Division 5 fine, with a maximum of $8,000.

| Section | What it says | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| s 14 | Only a licensed surveyor, someone under their supervision, or a trainee on an approved course may personally place a survey mark | Don't hammer in your own pegs |
| s 15 | The same restriction applies to carrying out a cadastral survey for fee or reward | A boundary survey for money needs a licensed surveyor |
| ss 16 to 18 | Nobody may hold themselves out as licensed or registered, or use those expressions, unless they are | Check the person, not the brochure |

Section 18 goes a step further. A person can't advertise a service using "licensed surveyor" or a derivative to describe someone who isn't licensed. That's why a firm should only describe an individual as licensed if they are.

The Surveyors Board SA puts the rule in one line: "In South Australia, only licensed surveyors can undertake a boundary survey or place a survey mark that defines a property boundary." To check a name, contact the Registrar (details below).

## Right of entry (section 46)

Section 46 lets a surveyor, or a person authorised in writing by a surveyor, enter any land "at any reasonable time" to carry out work connected with a survey. They can also take the action needed to do the survey properly, including excavating or breaking the surface "to the extent necessary to uncover or place a survey mark".

For you, that means the surveyor may need to come onto your block and sometimes onto a neighbour's. It's polite, and practical, to let neighbours know before the day. It also explains why a surveyor might lift a paver to find an old mark.

## Plans and who certifies them (section 47)

A plan or other document that the law says must be signed or certified by a surveyor must be signed or certified by "a surveyor who is a natural person". A company can't certify a plan. A named individual does.

That's another reason to ask for the surveyor's name when you request a quote. The same person's name is what you check with the Registrar.

## Survey marks and interference (section 52)

Section 52 is headed "Interference with survey marks". We haven't reproduced its text here, so read it on the legislation website if you need the detail. SA Government gives the owner's side in plain words: "By law, you cannot move or remove a survey mark and doing so can result in legal action and fines."

If a peg is in the way of a new fence or a driveway, don't shift it. Photograph it and tell the surveyor. Permanent survey marks in footpaths and kerbs are a separate thing, and SA Government says fines of up to $2,000 per mark can apply if they're disturbed, damaged, moved or removed. See [what to do when pegs go missing](/blog/survey-pegs-missing/).

## The Regulations: two rules worth knowing

**Regulation 17, identification surveys.** An identification survey is a cadastral survey of existing boundaries of land where no plan is otherwise required to be lodged in the Lands Titles Registration Office. A surveyor doing one "must comply with the provisions of the Lodgement of Boundary Identification Surveys Code". The Code is the Surveyors Board's, dated 17 March 2016. Under it, surveyors publicly record identification surveys by lodging a Plan for Information Purposes. Our page on [identification surveys](/identification-surveys/) covers the owner's side.

**Regulation 22, new allotments.** After a division into more than five allotments, the surveyor must place survey marks "not later than 30 days after completion of works for the provision of roads, drains or other services". It's a land-division rule, so it's something a developer's surveyor handles, not a homeowner.

## Who runs the system

Three bodies appear in the official wording.

- **The Institution of Surveyors Australia, South Australia Division (ISASA).** The Act gives licensing to the "Institution of Surveyors", and applications for a licence or registration go to it. The Board describes it as the statutory body responsible for controlling the training and conduct of licensed and registered surveyors.
- **The Surveyors Board of South Australia.** It's the management committee and performs the functions the Act prescribes. It has the power to investigate and prosecute unauthorised people who undertake property boundary surveys.
- **The Surveyor-General.** The Board's own description says the South Australian Government, through the Minister and the Surveyor-General, is responsible for issuing, monitoring and policing survey practice standards. The Surveyor-General also sits on the Board.

For a licence check, the Board's [public information page](https://www.surveyorsboardsa.org.au/public-info) explains how to reach the Registrar.

## What this means when you hire a surveyor

You don't have to memorise sections. Four habits cover most of it.

1. Get the surveyor's full name and check it with the Registrar.
2. Don't place, move or remove survey marks yourself.
3. Expect the surveyor to enter your land, and warn your neighbour.
4. Ask whether the survey will be lodged as a Plan for Information Purposes, and whether the quote includes it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the maximum fine under the Survey Act 1992?

The offences in sections 14 to 18, including placing a survey mark or carrying out a cadastral survey for fee or reward without a licence, and holding yourself out as licensed, carry a Division 5 fine. The Act's appendix lists the maximum as $8,000 as at the version date. A court can also disqualify a person from being licensed or registered.

### Can I place my own boundary pegs?

No. Section 14 says a person must not personally place a survey mark on or in land unless they're a licensed surveyor, acting under a licensed surveyor's supervision, or doing so on an approved training course.

### Does the Act say a surveyor can enter my property?

Yes. Section 46 allows a surveyor, or someone the surveyor authorises in writing, to enter land at any reasonable time to carry out survey work, and to take the action needed, including breaking the surface to uncover or place a mark. Tell your neighbour before the survey day.

### Who looks after the register of licensed surveyors?

The Surveyors Board of South Australia must maintain a register of Licensed and Registered Surveyors, and you check it by contacting the Registrar. The list is also published in the South Australian Government Gazette each July and January.
