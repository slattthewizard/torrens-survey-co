# Fence not on the boundary line: what it means in South Australia

You've had the boundary marked, and the fence isn't where it should be. Maybe it's 200 millimetres out. Maybe it's well over. The first thing you want to know is whether anyone has lost or gained land. The fence on its own doesn't change who owns what, so the real question is what happens now.

This article sets out how South Australian law treats a fence that's off the boundary, and when a survey is worth paying for. It's general information, not legal advice.

## The fence doesn't move the boundary

SA Government is clear. A fence's existing location does not change "the legal boundary; who legally owns the land; who owns the fence." The fence is jointly owned by you and your neighbour, and "The boundary can only be determined by a licensed surveyor."

So a fence that's stood for decades isn't evidence of where the line is. It's evidence of where someone once put a fence. The boundary stays where the title and registered plan put it, and a Licensed Surveyor works that out by measurement. Our article on the [Fences Act and the boundary fence](/blog/fences-act-sa-boundary-fence/) covers the wider fence rules.

## What the Fences Act says a court can do

The Fences Act 1975 (SA) gives the Magistrates Court specific powers where a fence isn't on the proper boundary. Under section 12(2) the court may decide "the line of fence to be adopted". It may also decide compensation "for loss of occupation of land as a result of the erection of a fence otherwise than upon the boundary", and it may "order the removal of a fence or any portion of a fence not erected upon the proper boundary."

That's a real power, but not a default outcome. The Legal Services Commission's booklet *Fences and the Law* (December 2023) says "a court is unlikely to order the relocation of a fence if the deviation from the boundary is minor." A few centimetres of drift is a different case from a fence that takes a strip of garden.

None of the sources we've read says what counts as minor, so don't assume your fence falls on one side or the other. A solicitor or the Legal Services Commission can tell you more.

## Compensation and the notice

The Act also deals with the situation before a fence goes up. A notice of intention to erect a fence must state, where the proposed line "encroaches into the land of the adjoining owner", whether the proponent proposes to pay compensation for loss of occupation, and if so the amount (section 5(2)(e)).

So a fence placed off the boundary on purpose isn't meant to happen quietly. The neighbour has 30 days after service to serve a cross-notice, and doesn't need to give a reason for objecting. If they don't object in time they're deemed to have agreed. Work can't start until the 30 days have passed or you have written consent, which is why the notice step matters.

## No adverse possession for an agreed off-line fence

A common worry: if a fence sits off the line for years, does the owner on the bigger side end up owning the strip?

Section 17 of the Fences Act covers one case. Where a dividing fence is built off the boundary under an agreement or court order, "neither of the adjoining owners shall, by reason of his occupation of the land enclosed by the fence, be deemed to be in adverse possession of any land of the other so as to acquire title to that land."

That speaks to off-line fences built by agreement or court order. It doesn't tell you how every situation plays out, and a fence with no agreement behind it isn't something we can answer. Our article on [adverse possession in SA](/blog/adverse-possession-sa/) goes into the difference between a fence problem and a title claim.

## When a survey is worth it

The Legal Services Commission's advice is that if there's a dispute about where a fence should go, "get a survey done which will show the precise location of the boundary."

A survey earns its fee when you and your neighbour each think the other has built over the line, when you're about to replace a fence and want to know where it belongs, or when you're planning a wall, shed or extension close to it. It also helps when you're buying or selling and the fence looks off, or when a fencing notice proposes a line you doubt.

It's harder to justify when a fence is a bit crooked and nobody disputes where it sits. Our article on [boundary fence disputes and surveys](/blog/boundary-fence-dispute-survey-sa/) walks through that call.

## Who pays for the survey

The Fences Act counts the cost of "any survey that is reasonably required for the purposes of the fencing work" as part of the cost of that work. But the Legal Services Commission says that if you both want the survey you can share the cost, "but if not, the person who arranges the survey will have to pay for it." A neighbour may object to paying if they think it isn't necessary, and a Magistrate then decides whether it was reasonably required.

Adelaide surveyors don't publish prices for boundary work. Trade Heroes' national guide puts boundary surveys at "$900 to $2,500" (updated 6 March 2026, GST not stated), which isn't an Adelaide price. A Licensed Surveyor quotes the job after seeing the title and the block.

## What to do next

1. Don't move, rebuild or pull down the fence on the strength of your own measurement.
2. Have a Licensed Surveyor mark the boundary. Check the licence with the Registrar at registrar@isasa.org.au or on (08) 8212 0343.
3. Talk to your neighbour with the result in hand.
4. If you can't agree, either neighbour can apply to the local Magistrates Court, and the application can be lodged through the CourtSA portal. The Legal Services Commission and Community Mediation Services can help beforehand.

The Commission suggests bringing "letters, quotes, photographs, surveys etc." to court, so keep your paperwork and the survey result.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does an old fence in the wrong place change my boundary?

No. SA Government says a fence's existing location doesn't change the legal boundary, who legally owns the land or who owns the fence. Only a licensed surveyor can determine the boundary position.

### Can a court order a fence to be moved?

Under section 12(2) of the Fences Act 1975, the Magistrates Court can decide the line of fence to be adopted, award compensation for loss of occupation, and order removal of a fence not on the proper boundary. The Legal Services Commission says a court is unlikely to order relocation if the deviation from the boundary is minor.

### Can I gain land because a fence has been in the wrong place for years?

Section 17 of the Fences Act says that where a dividing fence is built off the boundary under an agreement or court order, neither owner is deemed to be in adverse possession of the other's land. For any other situation, ask a solicitor or the Legal Services Commission.

### Do I need a survey if my fence is only slightly off?

Not always. The Legal Services Commission advises a survey where there's a dispute about where a fence should go, and says a court is unlikely to order relocation if the deviation is minor. If nobody disputes the position and you're not building near it, a survey may be more than you need.
