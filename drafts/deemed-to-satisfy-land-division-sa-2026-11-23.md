# Deemed to satisfy land division in SA: when a surveyor can give consent

If you've started reading about land division in South Australia, you've probably seen the phrase "deemed-to-satisfy". It sounds like jargon, and it is, but it points to something useful: some simple divisions can be assessed on a shorter path than others.

This article explains what the phrase means for a land division, who can give consent on that path, where it stops applying, and how it changes the planning fee. It's general information, not legal advice, and whether your division qualifies is a question for the council or a Licensed Surveyor.

## What deemed to satisfy means

Under SA's planning rules, deemed-to-satisfy and performance assessed are two different assessment paths. They carry different planning consent fees, and for a land division they can also change who gives consent and what plan is needed, as the sections below show.

For a land division, the relevant question is which of those two applies to your proposal. The answer depends on the zone and the details of the division. Don't take a lot size or layout from a website or a neighbour. Ask the council or a Licensed Surveyor to check your block against the Code, including any overlays, because we can't tell you from here.

## Who can give consent: regulation 22

Regulation 22 of the Planning, Development and Infrastructure (General) Regulations 2017 adds an option for simple divisions.

It says an "Accredited professional--surveyor may act as a relevant authority for the purposes of giving planning consent and consent under section 102(1)(c) or (d) of the Act in relation to development that is constituted solely by the division of 1 or more allotments and that may be assessed as deemed-to-satisfy development".

In plain terms, for a division that's only a division and that can be assessed as deemed-to-satisfy, an accredited surveyor can give both the planning consent and the land division consent. You still need the plan and the other pieces of the division.

Two points to keep straight. The surveyor here is acting as the relevant authority under the regulation, which is a different role from preparing the plan. And the regulation refers to an accredited professional, so ask whether the surveyor you're talking to holds that accreditation.

## Where it stops: the exceptions

Regulation 22 doesn't apply in every case. The exceptions are:

- where there are one or more minor variations
- where the division needs land to be vested in another entity
- where the division needs a public road to be created

If your division involves any of those, the shorter path isn't available and the application goes to the usual relevant authority. Raise any road or vesting of land with your surveyor early.

## What changes in the paperwork

Schedule 8 of the General Regulations lists what a land division application must include. Clause 7 sets out the plan requirements, including topographic features, registered easements and the position of buildings to be kept or removed.

Clause 7 "does not apply with respect to a division of land which is deemed-to-satisfy development". For those divisions, "Only a 'final plan' is required".

That's a real difference for the owner: the clause 7 plan requirements don't apply to a deemed-to-satisfy division. It doesn't mean there's no survey work, because the final plan still has to be prepared and signed or certified by a surveyor (Survey Act, s 47).

For divisions with more than five allotments or a new road, clause 7(3) separately requires contours "vouched for by a licensed surveyor". That's another reason the exceptions above matter.

## The planning consent fee

The Planning, Development and Infrastructure (Fees) Notice 2026 sets the planning consent fees from 1 July 2026. Two items matter here:

| Assessment path | Fee item | 2026-27 fee |
|---|---|---|
| Deemed-to-satisfy, "in any other case" | 6(a)(ii) | $250.00 |
| Performance assessed | 6(b) | "$299.00 or 0.125% of the total development cost up to a maximum of $200,000, whichever is the greater" |

Item 6(a)(i) is a lower fee of $151.00 for a deemed-to-satisfy development costing no more than $10,000.

The gap between $250.00 and $299.00 is small. The reason to care about the path isn't the planning fee. It's what follows from it: who can give consent and what plan is needed. Other fees still apply on top, such as the lodgement fee, the Commission's certificate of approval fee and the Land Services SA plan fees, and which ones depend on the application. This article doesn't add them up.

## What stays the same either way

The shorter path doesn't remove the rest of the process. LSSA's steps still run: lodgement with Land Services SA, examination of the plan and the dealings, deposit and issue of title. SA Water still assesses every proposed division, and its assessment is valid for 60 business days once delivered.

Government fees also still apply. For 2026-27 the SA Water assessment is $480.00 and the certificate of approval fee under section 138 is $1,229.00.

The survey side doesn't change either. The Surveyors Board of South Australia says that "In South Australia, only licensed surveyors can undertake a boundary survey or place a survey mark that defines a property boundary." Whichever path your division takes, check your surveyor's licence with the Registrar or the Gazette list before you engage them.

## What to ask before you commit

You don't need to work out the assessment path yourself. Put these questions to the council and your Licensed Surveyor:

1. Can my proposed division be assessed as deemed-to-satisfy in my zone, and are any overlays involved?
2. Does the division need land vested in another entity or a public road?
3. Are there any minor variations in the design?
4. Is the surveyor you're talking to an accredited professional who can act as the relevant authority under regulation 22?
5. If the answer is performance assessed instead, what changes in the plans and fees?

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does deemed to satisfy mean for a land division?

It's one of the assessment paths under SA's planning rules. It's distinct from performance assessment, and the two carry different planning consent fees. Whether yours qualifies depends on the zone and the details of the proposal, so ask the council or a Licensed Surveyor to check.

### Can a surveyor really give planning consent?

In limited cases, yes. Regulation 22 of the General Regulations lets an accredited professional who is a surveyor act as a relevant authority for planning consent and consent under section 102(1)(c) or (d) of the Planning, Development and Infrastructure Act 2016. It applies to a division that's deemed-to-satisfy and doesn't involve minor variations, land vested in another entity or a public road.

### Is the planning fee lower for a deemed-to-satisfy division?

Slightly. For 2026-27, the deemed-to-satisfy fee in item 6(a)(ii) is $250.00, and the performance-assessed fee in item 6(b) is $299.00 or 0.125% of the total development cost, whichever is greater. Other government fees apply on top of either.

### Does a deemed-to-satisfy division still need a survey?

Yes. A plan of division still has to be signed or certified by a surveyor, and in South Australia only a licensed surveyor can place a survey mark that defines a property boundary. What changes is the plan required at the consent stage, because only a final plan is needed.
