# Can a surveyor enter my property in South Australia?

Someone in a high-vis vest is standing at your fence with a tripod, and they say they need to come onto your block. Or a letter has arrived saying a surveyor will be visiting. You're entitled to ask what's going on, and it helps to know what the law lets them do.

This article explains the right of entry in the Survey Act 1992 (SA), what a surveyor can and can't do on your land, and how to check who you're dealing with. It's general information, not legal advice.

## What the Survey Act says

The source is section 46. It reads: "A surveyor, or a person authorised in writing by a surveyor, may-- (a) at any reasonable time, enter any land for the purposes of carrying out work in connection with a survey; and (b) take such action as is necessary to enable the survey to be carried out effectively (including excavating or breaking the surface of land to the extent necessary to uncover or place a survey mark)."

A few points follow from that wording.

- It covers "any land", not only the land of whoever hired the surveyor. A survey of your neighbour's block can need access to yours.
- It applies "at any reasonable time". The Act doesn't define that, but it's not a licence to turn up whenever suits.
- The person at your gate may not be the surveyor. Someone "authorised in writing by a surveyor" can enter too, so it's fair to ask who they work for.
- The power is tied to "work in connection with a survey". It isn't a general right to wander about.

## Breaking surfaces to find a mark

The Act expressly allows "excavating or breaking the surface of land to the extent necessary to uncover or place a survey mark." SA Government says the same in plain terms: "By law, surveyors can break any surface to locate a marker, including driveways, footpaths and landscaped areas."

The limit is in the words "to the extent necessary". A surveyor who needs to find a buried mark under a garden bed or a paved path can dig for it. The Act doesn't hand them licence to dig up more than the task needs.

If there's a particular surface you're worried about, such as a lawn you've just laid or paving you've just paid for, say so before the day. Whether the surveyor can work around it depends on the job, and that's a fair thing to ask. ## What you can and can't do about it

You can ask questions. You're entitled to know who's on your land and what the survey is for. Ask when they plan to come, how long it might take and where they'll need to go.

You can't move the marks they place. SA Government says "By law, you cannot move or remove a survey mark made by a licenced surveyor", and that doing so "can result in legal action and fines." That applies after the surveyor has left, too. If a peg sits in your garden bed or beside your shed, it stays where it is.

If you have a real concern about how a surveyor has behaved, start with the Surveyors Board of South Australia, which performs the functions set out in the Survey Act. A solicitor or the Legal Services Commission can tell you where you stand legally.

## Practical courtesy that helps everyone

The law gives a surveyor the right to come in. That doesn't make an unannounced arrival the best way to run a job. A few habits keep things smooth.

Tell your neighbours before the surveyor arrives. If the survey is yours, a quick note or chat means nobody is startled by a stranger in the back yard. Expect the surveyor to want access along the fence lines, because that's where the corners and marks usually are.

Make sure gates open. A locked side gate, a dog in the yard or a shed hard against the boundary changes the job, so mention it when you ask for the quote. Clear anything stacked against the fence, like pavers or timber, and ask for a time window and whether someone needs to be home.

If the survey is part of a dispute, say so when you ask for the quote. Our guide to the [boundary survey](/blog/boundary-survey-south-australia/) explains what the surveyor will ask.

Keep the conversation civil even if you're unhappy about the visit. The surveyor is doing work the Act allows, and a short chat about gates, pets and access usually settles things faster than an argument at the fence. If the work is for a neighbour's survey and you have questions about it, the neighbour who hired the surveyor is the right first call.

## Checking the person is a licensed surveyor

In South Australia, only a licensed surveyor can place a survey mark that defines a property boundary, and the Surveyors Board says surveys done by unlicensed practitioners "do not have any legal standing." The Board also advises: "If you engage a surveying firm, check that the company uses a surveyor who is licensed in South Australia and legally entitled to define the boundaries of your property."

To check, email the Registrar at registrar@isasa.org.au or call (08) 8212 0343. The Board says Licensed and Registered Surveyors are published in the South Australian Government Gazette, updated each July and January. The [Surveyors Board SA public information page](https://www.surveyorsboardsa.org.au/public-info) explains how.

Ask the person at your gate for their name and who they work for. If they're working under a surveyor, ask for that surveyor's name and check it. The Act refers to a person "authorised in writing", so asking to see that authority is reasonable. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a surveyor come onto my land without asking?

Section 46 of the Survey Act 1992 lets a surveyor, or a person authorised in writing by a surveyor, enter any land at a reasonable time for work in connection with a survey. In practice a considerate surveyor will tell you first, and it's reasonable to ask who they are and what the survey is for.

### Can a surveyor dig up my driveway or garden?

The Act lets them excavate or break the surface "to the extent necessary to uncover or place a survey mark". SA Government says surveyors can break any surface to locate a marker, including driveways, footpaths and landscaped areas. The words "to the extent necessary" set the limit.

### Can I move a survey peg that's in my way?

No. SA Government says that by law you cannot move or remove a survey mark made by a licenced surveyor, and doing so can result in legal action and fines. Raise the problem with the surveyor or a solicitor instead.

### How do I check the person is a licensed surveyor?

Email the Registrar at registrar@isasa.org.au or call (08) 8212 0343. The Surveyors Board SA also says Licensed and Registered Surveyors are published in the Government Gazette each July and January. Get the surveyor's full name before you check.
