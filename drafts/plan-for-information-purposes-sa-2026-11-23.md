# Plan for Information Purposes in SA: what it is and why it matters

After a boundary survey, most owners expect pegs in the ground. Fewer expect a plan, and fewer still know it may be recorded in a public system. That plan is called a Plan for Information Purposes, or PIP, and it's what turns a surveyor's visit into something the next owner and the next surveyor can use.

This guide explains what a PIP is, how it differs from the plans used to divide land, what the pegs do and don't mean, and what to ask before you accept a quote. It's general information, not legal advice.

## Where the PIP comes from

The Survey Regulations 2020 deal with identification surveys. Regulation 17 defines an identification survey as a cadastral survey of existing boundaries of land where no plan is otherwise required by law to be lodged in the Lands Titles Registration Office (LTRO). A surveyor carrying one out "must comply with the provisions of the Lodgement of Boundary Identification Surveys Code".

That Code is "the Code of Practice--Lodgement of Boundary Identification Surveys, prepared by the Surveyors Board of South Australia, 17 March 2016". Under it, surveyors are "publicly recording their identification surveys through lodging a Plan for Information Purposes", according to the Surveyor-General's Cadastral Survey Guidelines.

So a PIP isn't an optional extra a surveyor invented. It's the way the Code has identification surveys recorded.

## What a PIP is, and what it isn't

The same guidelines say: "While Plans for Information Purposes are not examined by the LTRO, they are certified as having been carried out in accord with the Survey Act 1992". Two points follow.

- **It's certified.** A licensed surveyor stands behind the survey. The Act says a plan that must be certified by a surveyor has to be certified by a surveyor who is a natural person, so a named individual signs.
- **It isn't examined.** The LTRO doesn't check it the way it checks a plan of division. A PIP isn't a plan of division and doesn't create new allotments.

It also doesn't settle your boundary for all time. It records one licensed surveyor's identification of it, done in accord with the Act.

## PIP versus a plan of division

People mix the two up because both are plans lodged after a survey. They do different jobs.

| | Plan for Information Purposes | Plan of division |
|---|---|---|
| Used for | Recording an identification survey of existing boundaries | Creating new allotments |
| Examined by LTRO | No | Yes, then deposited |
| Creates new allotments | No | Yes |

A land division involves planning consent, a certificate of approval and examination and deposit of the plan, which Land Services SA says can take "many months, sometimes even years". A PIP sits outside all that. See [land division surveys](/land-division-surveys/) if your goal is to split a block.

## When a PIP comes into play

A PIP follows an identification survey, so it turns up whenever an owner needs the existing boundary confirmed. The government sources list the usual triggers: you and your neighbour don't agree on the boundary location, you're building on or near a boundary, or you're buying or selling and want to know about any encroachments involving the neighbouring properties. SA Government also points to a licensed surveyor to confirm whether an encroachment has occurred.

## Why a PIP matters to owners

A recorded survey means the work doesn't vanish when the pegs do. Pegs get dug up, paved over and knocked out by fencing crews, as we explain in our guide to [missing pegs](/blog/survey-pegs-missing/). A lodged PIP is a public record that the identification survey was done.

That helps in three ways, and none of them need you to read a plan yourself. Your surveyor, your designer or a buyer's surveyor can do that for you.

1. **A future surveyor has something to start from.** They can see what an earlier surveyor found and placed, rather than starting from nothing.
2. **You can show your neighbour, buyer or designer what was done.** It's documented, not remembered.
3. **The survey was done to a standard.** Because the Code requires a surveyor to comply with it, the plan reflects that process.

A PIP isn't a guarantee. It's evidence, and it only helps if it exists.

## What the pegs mean

The Surveyor-General's guidelines describe pegs placed on identification surveys as ones that "simply represent the surveyor's opinion as to the position of the boundary and are subject to any errors of survey or judgement which the surveyor may commit". That's standard language, not a reason to distrust a survey. It's a reason to hire a licensed person and check them.

Only licensed surveyors can place survey marks that define a property boundary. By law you can't move or remove a survey mark made by a licensed surveyor, and SA Government says doing so can result in legal action and fines. If a peg is in the way, photograph it and call the surveyor. Our [re-establishment survey guide](/blog/re-establishment-survey/) covers what to expect.

If a neighbour disputes the result, a PIP doesn't settle it. Talk to the Legal Services Commission, Community Mediation Services or a solicitor.

## Does your quote include lodging it?

Ask. Pegging a boundary and recording the survey could be quoted as separate items, so don't assume either way. Adelaide firms quote each job and don't publish survey prices, so there's no standard answer, and we can't tell you what it costs.

Put these questions in writing.

- Will the survey be lodged as a Plan for Information Purposes?
- Is lodging included in the quoted price, or extra?
- Will I receive a copy of the plan?
- Who is the surveyor who will certify it?

You can check that surveyor's licence with the Registrar, as the Board's [public information page](https://www.surveyorsboardsa.org.au/public-info) explains. Our [land survey quote checklist](/blog/land-survey-quote-checklist/) has more.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who lodges a Plan for Information Purposes?

The surveyor who carries out the identification survey. Under the Code the Surveyors Board prepared, surveyors publicly record their identification surveys by lodging a PIP, and it's certified as carried out in accord with the Survey Act 1992. Ask whether lodging it is included in your quote.

### Is a PIP the same as a deposited plan?

No. A PIP records an identification survey of existing boundaries and isn't examined by the Lands Titles Registration Office. A plan of division creates new allotments and goes through examination and deposit.

### Does every boundary survey get a PIP?

Under the Code, identification surveys are recorded by lodging one, and a surveyor doing an identification survey must comply with it. Other kinds of survey may not. Ask your surveyor which type of job yours is and whether a plan will be lodged.

### Is a PIP a guarantee that the boundary is right?

No. The Surveyor-General's guidelines say pegs on an identification survey represent the surveyor's opinion of the boundary and are subject to errors of survey or judgement. A lodged PIP makes that opinion a public, certified record, but it isn't a guarantee.
