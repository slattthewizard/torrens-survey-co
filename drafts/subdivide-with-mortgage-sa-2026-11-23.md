# Subdividing land with a mortgage in South Australia

If your block has a mortgage on it, the lender is part of your subdivision whether you've told them or not. That doesn't mean you can't divide the land. It means there's one more party whose agreement is needed, and it's worth knowing when and how that comes up before you pay for plans.

This article explains whose consent a division needs, what lenders usually require, and who arranges it all. It's general information, not legal advice, and your conveyancer or solicitor is the person to confirm the detail for your title.

## Why a mortgage matters to a land division

A mortgage is a registered interest on your certificate of title. A subdivision creates new allotments with new titles, so the lender is one of the parties whose agreement the paperwork asks for.

Land Services SA (LSSA) puts it like this. The land division dealing needs consent "by not only the registered proprietors but also any registered interests on the Certificate of Title". The registered proprietors are the owners on the title. A mortgagee is one of the registered interests.

What the lender wants in return, and on what terms, is between you and them. We can't say how any particular lender decides.

## How to see what's registered on your title

Before you do anything else, look at the certificate of title. It lists registered interests, including mortgages and easements. Our post on the [certificate of title in SA](/blog/certificate-of-title-sa/) explains what it records and what it doesn't.

Two things to check:

- **Every registered interest, not just the mortgage.** A second mortgage or another registered interest also needs to be dealt with. Each one is another consent.
- **Every owner on the title.** All the registered proprietors must consent, including co-owners.

If you're not sure what a line on the title means, ask your conveyancer or solicitor to read it with you.

## What lenders usually require

LSSA's fact sheet says: "most financial institutions will require the plan to be 'deposited' (when the allotments are legally created) before settlements can occur."

That matters if your plan is to sell one of the new lots, because settlement is when money moves. If your lender has that requirement, a sale can't settle until the plan is deposited. If you're selling the back block to pay for work on the front, your finance depends on the plan being deposited, which is one of the last steps.

So there are two points where the lender is involved: consent while the application is prepared, and deposit before settlement. Because the whole process can take "many months, sometimes even years" in LSSA's words, you'll want the lender's position clear from the start.

## Who arranges the consents

LSSA says the application for a division "is in the form of a legal LSSA dealing" and "A Registered Conveyancer or Solicitor prepares the application." Arranging the consents of the registered interests is part of that work.

Land Services SA strongly recommends engaging a licensed surveyor and a conveyancer or solicitor. The surveyor prepares the plan and lodges it electronically. The conveyancer or solicitor handles the dealing and the consents. You're in the middle, signing, so it helps to know who's doing what.

### What the conveyancing can cost

Conveyancing Centre, an SA firm, states: "We charge only $660.00 (including GST) to prepare and lodge any of the above forms and only $88 for each consent arranged (including GST)." The page is undated, so confirm the figures with whoever you engage. Fees from other firms will differ, and the number of consents on your title changes the total.

That's one firm's published fee, not a price for your division. Ask for a quote in writing that says how many consents it covers.

## Where the lender fits in the sequence

LSSA's steps run from a council enquiry, through approval by the State Commission Assessment Panel, to lodgement with LSSA, examination of the plan and the dealings, deposit and issue of title. The lender's consent belongs with the dealing, which LSSA says is lodged with the plan and the necessary consents once the Certificate of Approval has been given.

That means the consent doesn't have to be signed on day one. It also means you shouldn't leave the lender until the end. If a lender says no, or wants conditions, you'd rather know before you've paid for a plan and the planning fees. For 2026-27 the certificate of approval fee alone is $1,229.00, and that's one fee among several.

## What to have ready

Whoever you speak to will ask for the same few things, so it saves time to gather them first:

- A recent copy of the certificate of title, showing every registered proprietor and every registered interest.
- The loan details, including the lender's name and your account or reference number.
- A rough idea of what you want to create, such as two lots, and whether you plan to sell one.
- The Licensed Surveyor's draft plan, once you have it.

None of this needs to be perfect. A conveyancer or solicitor can tell you what's missing.

## Talk to your lender early

The simplest advice is also the most useful. Contact your lender before you commit to plans, and ask what they need from you. Questions worth asking:

1. Do they need to consent, and what do they need to see to give it?
2. Do they want the plan deposited before any lot is sold?
3. How will the mortgage be dealt with across the new titles?
4. How long do they usually take to respond, and who's the contact?

You'll get a more reliable answer from your lender than from any website, including this one. Write down what they say, and give a copy to your conveyancer or solicitor.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I subdivide my land if it has a mortgage?

The mortgage doesn't rule it out, but it adds a step. Land Services SA says the division dealing needs consent from the registered proprietors and from any registered interests on the certificate of title, and a mortgagee is one of those. Talk to your lender and your conveyancer or solicitor before you commit to plans.

### Does the lender have to agree before I lodge the plan?

Their consent is needed as part of the division dealing, which goes to Land Services SA with the plan once the Commission's certificate of approval is given. Your conveyancer or solicitor prepares the dealing and arranges the consents, so ask them when the lender should be approached.

### Can I sell one of the new lots before the plan is deposited?

LSSA says most financial institutions require the plan to be deposited before settlements can occur. Deposit is when the allotments are legally created. Check what your own lender requires, and what your contract of sale says, with your conveyancer or solicitor.

### Who arranges the lender's consent?

Normally your conveyancer or solicitor. They prepare the application and arrange the consents of the registered interests. Conveyancing Centre, one SA firm, publishes $88 (including GST) for each consent arranged and $660.00 (including GST) per form, and other firms charge differently.
